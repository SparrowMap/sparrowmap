RavenMap

A camera network the neighbourhood owns.

“And ye shall be brought before governors and kings for my sake, for a testimony against them.”

Matthew 10:18

What this is

Private companies sell automated licence plate readers to police departments. The cameras sit on public roads, record every vehicle that passes, and the resulting history is searchable by the agency that bought the subscription. The public pays for it and cannot see it.

RavenMap is the same technology pointed the other way. Volunteers put a camera in a window or on a porch facing a public road. The camera reads what passes. The results go on a map that anyone can open, with no account, no agency, and no subscription.

The line we drew

A network like this can be built two ways, and only one of them is worth building. If every plate is public and searchable, then anyone can look up an ex-partner's week with photographs. That is not accountability. That is a stalking service with a civic-sounding name.

So RavenMap keeps two tiers, and the split is enforced in code rather than in a policy document.

Public tier

  • Government vehicles, local and federal
  • Plate stored readable and searchable
  • Full history, kept indefinitely
  • Every operator decision on it is logged publicly
  • Searching it is free and never logged

Private tier

  • Everybody else
  • Plate hashed at the camera, never stored readable
  • Deleted after a short window
  • No search path exists, by design

The reasoning is old and simple. A publicly owned vehicle, doing public work, on a public road, is a public record, the same way a council minute is. A private person driving to work is not.

Four things that are true about how it is built

No video ever leaves the camera. Recognition runs on the device. What reaches the hub is a few hundred bytes of structured claim and one cropped still. There is no stream to intercept, subpoena or leak, because there is no stream.

Snapshots are crops of the vehicle, not the frame. The pedestrian on the sidewalk, the house number behind, the kid in the yard, none of them are in a tight crop of a car's rear end.

The hash key rotates. Private plates are stored as a keyed hash so the same car still re-identifies across cameras. That key is rotated on a schedule, so after a rotation the same car hashes differently and old trails cannot be joined to new ones. This caps how long anyone can follow a private citizen including whoever runs the database.

Nothing gets published on plate text alone. A vehicle only enters the public tier at high confidence and only when something other than the plate agrees, because a single misread should never be able to make a stranger public.

What it is not

RavenMap does not identify people, only vehicles. It does not do face recognition, and the crop rule is there specifically to make faces hard to collect even by accident. It has no enforcement function, no alerting to any agency, and no hotlist that a stranger can add your car to.

It will also be wrong sometimes. Every classification on the map shows the evidence behind it rather than only the verdict, so a bad call is visible and arguable instead of authoritative.

Why a sparrow

RavenMap is a fork and continuation of SparrowMap. The SparrowMap name and history are preserved here as provenance, not as a claim of affiliation with the upstream project. RavenMap's raven name follows the older image of Huginn and Muninn, Odin's ravens: observation carried back with judgment, while the architecture and project identity remain distinct.

Matthew 10 is Jesus sending people out with a warning that they will be handed to councils and dragged in front of governors. In the middle of it: “there is nothing covered, that shall not be revealed”, and then “what ye hear in the ear, that preach ye upon the housetops.” A porch camera is close to a literal reading of that instruction.

Then the sparrow, which was the cheapest animal in the market, sold two for a farthing and the fifth thrown in free. The point of the line is that even the creature nobody bothered to count is not unwatched when it falls. Which is the argument for the two tiers, in one image: the powerful get watched, and the ordinary person is the one whose fall nobody gets to sell.

Found a bug, or want to help?

The whole system is open source, so a claim you doubt can be checked against the code that makes it. If something is wrong — a vehicle classified incorrectly, a broken page, a number that does not add up — report it where it can be fixed in the open: report a bug on GitHub →

For anything else — running a camera, a question, or a correction that should not be public — sparrowmap@icloud.com.

It costs $200.48 a month in servers to run, nearly all of it the one machine that serves the map and reads several thousand public traffic cameras. If you want to help with that: buymeacoffee.com/sparrowmap. It goes to a person, not a foundation, and nothing on the map works differently either way. The bills are broken down on the status page, and the support page carries the growth numbers, what the server has left, and the specific hardware this needs next with a cost against each.

The code is open source (AGPL-3.0): run your own instance, please do. The SparrowMap name and mark are the project's identity, not part of that licence — a fork is welcome, under its own name.
